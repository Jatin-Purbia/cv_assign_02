Cost-Efficient Segmentation under a Compute Budget
Objective
Create a pipeline that labels every pixel of a photo as either main object or background, using
on average at most 50 ms of CPU time on a single core per photo. You can follow Track A,
which uses classical methods (clustering followed by a graph cut), or Track B, which uses a
small CNN. Your goal is to make the Cost-Efficient Segmentation (CES) score, defined below,
as high as possible. No boilerplate code is provided for this assignment.
You are
responsible for structuring the pipeline yourself.
1. Data Collection
a. DUTS Dataset: Download DUTS from http://saliencydetection.net/duts/. Its
masks mark the main object or objects of each photo in white. It has 10,553 training
photos (DUTS-TR) and 5,019 test photos (DUTS-TE). Split DUTS-TR into your own
training and validation sets and make all design choices on the validation set. Never use
DUTS-TE for training, tuning or deciding when to stop training.
b. Resizing: Resize every photo to 256 × 256 with cv2.INTER AREA and every mask with
cv2.INTER NEAREST. A mask pixel is object if its value is at least 128. All metrics are
computed on these 256 × 256 masks. Squashing a photo that is not square is intended.
c. Your Own Photos: Take 5 to 10 phone photos around your college or hostel, each
showing one clear everyday object such as a water bottle, a bag, a cycle, a chair, a pair
of shoes, a plant or a campus cat. Together they should cover at least three of these five
conditions: both indoor and outdoor scenes, both daylight and tube light, both near and
far objects, a cluttered background, and partial occlusion. Resize each photo with the
rule above, then draw its mask on the resized photo with any tool you like, such as Paint,
GIMP, LabelMe or your own script. Save masks as PNGs with the object in white (255)
and the background in black (0). If your tool leaves grey pixels along the edges, the 128
threshold above decides them. Use these photos only for testing.

3. Implementation Requirements
Track A: Classical Computer Vision (from scratch)
You may use NumPy or PyTorch, for example autograd to train your region model, but write
the clustering and the max flow yourself.
• Clustering into Regions: Describe each pixel by its colour and its (x, y) position,
optionally with texture filter responses, so that clusters form compact regions. Group the
pixels with K-Means, a GMM trained with EM, or Mean-Shift, choosing the fastest one
that suits your pipeline.
• Region Probabilities: Use your training set to learn a probability Pp that region p is
object, and clip it to [0.001, 0.999]. For example, you could record how often each image
position is object, build colour histograms of object and background pixels, or measure
how much a region touches the image border, and then combine such clues with a logistic
regression you train yourself.
• Graph Cut: Build a graph with one node per region, a source node for object and a
sink node for background. Connect the source to region p with capacity −log(1−Pp) and
region p to the sink with capacity −log Pp, both times the region’s pixel count. Cutting
the sink edge keeps p on the source side and pays the object cost −log Pp, and cutting
the source edge pays the background cost. Two regions touch if any of their pixels are
directly above, below, left or right of each other. Join touching regions in both directions
with an edge of weight λ Wpq times their shared border length (the number of touching
pixel pairs), where Wpq = exp(−∥f(p)−f(q)∥2/2σ2
d) and f is the mean colour of a region,
so that similar neighbours prefer the same label. Tune λ and σd on your validation set,
starting from λ = 1 and, for each image, σ2

its touching regions, plus a small constant such as 10−6 so that it is never zero. Find the
minimum cut with your own max flow algorithm, such as Edmonds-Karp. Regions on the
source side of the cut are object.
• Efficiency: Clustering and max flow in Python over all 65,536 pixels will not fit in 50
ms. Instead, cluster a smaller copy of the photo (for example 64 × 64) into about 100 to
200 regions, run the graph cut on those regions, and enlarge the final mask to 256 × 256.

Track B: Fully Convolutional Network (from scratch in PyTorch)
• Network: Build a small fully convolutional network (FCN) as covered in class, from
convolutions (including any of 1 × 1 and depthwise separable ones), pooling, batch norm,
ReLU, and transpose convolutions or bilinear upsampling. The encoder shrinks the image,
the decoder brings it back to full size, and skip connections pass encoder features to the
decoder, either by adding them as in FCN-8s or by concatenating them as in U-Net. Train
it from scratch with pixel wise cross entropy, optionally adding a Soft Dice loss.
• Efficiency: Choose the size of the network and the resolution it works at so that the
pipeline stays under 50 ms.
Evaluation Metrics: Both Tracks (from scratch)
• mIoU: Add up one 2 × 2 confusion matrix over all images in a set, compute the IoU
(intersection over union) for background and for object, and average the two.
• Boundary F1: BF1 checks whether the outline of your mask is in the right place.
1. A boundary pixel is an object pixel with a background pixel directly above, below,
left or right of it. Pixels outside the image count as background.
2. A predicted boundary pixel is correct if a true boundary pixel lies within its 5 × 5
window, which allows for small annotation errors. A true boundary pixel is found if
a predicted boundary pixel lies within its 5 × 5 window.
3. Precision P is the fraction of predicted boundary pixels that are correct, and recall
R is the fraction of true boundary pixels that are found. BF1 = 2PR/(P + R), or 0
if P + R = 0.
4. Compute BF1 per image and average over the set. If neither mask has boundary
pixels, BF1 is 1, and if only one does, it is 0. Looping over every pixel and its window
in Python is too slow for 5,019 images, so find a faster way to do this check.
